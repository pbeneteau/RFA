## Verdict

**The literature does not confirm the ladder; it re-ranks it and adds one rung the ladder is missing.** Three findings do the re-ranking. First, the field's convergent result (a 2026 position paper, arXiv:2608.18092, and the only system with a head-to-head evaluation, CoAgent, arXiv:2606.15376) is that BOTH of the ladder's poles are the wrong primitive for minutes-long agent turns: pessimistic locks serialize away the parallelism (CoAgent measured 2PL at roughly 1.04x speedup with 0.81 deadlocks per trial) and optimistic abort-and-retry burns the reasoning (OCC came out 0.93x, slower than serial, at 1.83x token cost); what wins in measurement is a third primitive, notify-the-agent-and-let-it-repair (within 5 percent of serial correctness at 1.4x speedup, 1.15x tokens). Rung 5's "conflicts become explicit and sequential" at merge time is the abort pole wearing git clothing, and AgenticFlict (arXiv:2604.03551, 107,026 simulated merges of real AI-agent PRs) prices it: 27.67 percent of agent PRs conflict, a conflicting PR averages 4.36 files and 11.36 conflict regions, and the rate is churn-dependent (about 9.9 percent at 2-line median churn, near 30 percent at 25 lines). Merge-back is the expensive tier, not the default tier. Second, the SWE test-time-compute line (Large Language Monkeys, CodeMonkeys, DEI) names a rung the ladder skips entirely: N independent candidate runs of the SAME task with one selected and the rest discarded have no shared-state conflict by construction, and the measured bottleneck moves to selection (CodeMonkeys: 69.8 percent coverage collapses to 57.4 percent after selection; DEI: a 54.3 percent oracle union yields 34.3 percent selected). RFA already owns the missing piece, verification authority (wire 10.4). Third, CapLease (arXiv:2608.01710) exposes a hazard the ladder never mentions and parallelism multiplies: across 10,152 agent trajectories, 39.8 percent of uncertain execution outcomes (lost acknowledgement 58 percent, timeout 46 percent) induced a semantically equivalent re-proposal of an already-authorized action. RFA's per-call approval card is stronger than the single-use-token baseline that paper defeats, but the repo has no canonical-action identity across cards and no idempotency contract for gated tools (negative grep on `src/bridge.ts` for dedup/idempotency, 2026-08-25), so a crash or retry presents the same action to the human twice and two approvals mean two executions. One transfer the scout oversold dies on the paper's own honesty: S-Bus-style hub-side read-set reconstruction is REJECTED for pack workspaces, because S-Bus itself measures only 26.1 percent of an agent's single-step references as HTTP-observable, and an RFA resident's file reads never traverse the hub at all.

## 1. The field's frame: neither locks nor OCC, and why (position paper + CoAgent)

`papers/position-mas-concurrency-control.pdf` (arXiv:2608.18092, fetched 2026-08-25) argues the defining property of the workload is temporal asymmetry: an agent "transaction" spans minutes of inference, so a pessimistic lock held for the turn destroys the parallelism that motivated concurrency in the first place, while an optimistic abort throws away minutes of paid reasoning rather than microseconds of CPU. The paper's failure catalog (stale reads, lost updates, stale corrections, action-message desynchronization) is drawn from measured systems, not hypotheticals: it cites CAID-style worktree isolation lifting multi-agent SWE resolve to 63.3 percent versus 55.5 percent unisolated (with unisolated multi-agent UNDERPERFORMING a 57.2 percent single agent, meaning naive parallelism is worse than none), and a contention benchmark attributing 67.1 percent of multi-agent failures to concurrency effects. Its constructive claim is that the conflict signal should be fed back to the agent semantically instead of resolved below it.

`papers/coagent-concurrency-control-multi-agent.pdf` (arXiv:2606.15376, fetched 2026-08-25) is the head-to-head test of that claim, and it is the single most transferable evidence in this dimension. On 10 contended multi-agent workloads it compares uncoordinated execution (passes 13 percent of trials), two-phase locking (0.81 deadlocks per trial, about 1.04x speedup), classic OCC (0.95 aborts per trial, 0.93x, i.e. slower than serial, at 1.83x token cost), and its own mechanism: declared per-tool read/write footprints, a pre-order on tool calls, and, on a detected conflicting write, a NOTIFICATION into the running agent's context so the agent itself re-executes only the operations premised on the stale value. That lands within 5 percent of serial correctness at 1.4x speedup and 1.15x token cost. Two honest caveats from the PDF: the notification path misjudged relevance in about 5 percent of trials (the LLM is the adjudicator, and it is fallible), and the mechanism requires registered tools with declared footprints plus a commit hook, which is real integration work.

Transfer to the ladder. Rung 5 as drafted performs the conflict resolution after run completion, outside the run's context, serially. Both papers say that is the most expensive point to resolve it: the context that could repair cheaply is gone. The fix is not to abandon merge-back but to move the conflict signal into the run as a tool-result while it still holds context (recommendation 1). RFA's `canUseTool` interception point (`src/resident.ts:623`) is exactly where CoAgent's footprint check and notification would sit, and the pack's declared tool surface (`src/posture.ts`, CLAUDE.md "tools are the declaration") is the coarse ancestor of CoAgent's declared footprints (recommendation 9).

## 2. An anomaly vocabulary that is checkable (TLA+ paper)

`papers/verified-concurrency-anomalies-tla.pdf` (arXiv:2606.17182, fetched 2026-08-25) formalizes four agent-specific anomalies in TLA+: stale-generation (output generated from a superseded read), phantom-tool (a tool effect visible to a run that should not see it), causal-cascade (a stale value propagating through downstream turns), and tool-effect reordering. It checks them against the actual coordination internals of LangGraph (reducer merge) and AutoGen (ETag layer), and connects the stale-generation anomaly to a silent lost update reported in ByteDance's deer-flow. Two results matter for RFA. First, mitigation cost is bounded and small at the validation end: SSI-style commit-time validation added about 8 percent tokens in their measurements, while pessimistic locking cost 1.6-2.3x latency; this kills the argument that any concurrency control is unaffordably expensive for agent turns. Second, anomaly frequency is workload-shaped, not constant: their pilot measured stale-generation in 1 percent, 35 percent, and 100 percent of runs across three workloads. A per-rung anomaly table in the spec (recommendation 5) is therefore honest in a way a single "safe/unsafe" bit is not: rung 3 (read-only packs) admits stale-generation against a changing knowledge clone but no lost updates; rung 4 (scratch dirs) admits phantom-tool only through the shared /memories surface; and so on.

## 3. The S-Bus transfer dies on S-Bus's own numbers

`papers/sbus-read-set-reconstruction.pdf` (arXiv:2605.17076 v2, fetched 2026-08-25) is middleware that reconstructs each agent's read set server-side from HTTP GET traffic (a per-agent DeliveryLog) and runs OCC at commit with zero in-agent code. The formal work is serious (TLC exhaustive at N=3 over 20.7M states, TLAPS obligations discharged, Dafny lemmas), and on its intended topology (each agent owns a distinct write shard, reads shared reference shards) it measured 0 Type-I corruptions across 427,308 commit attempts under active contention. But the paper's own scope section is the verdict for RFA: only 26.1 percent of an agent's single-step state references were observable at the HTTP layer on its principal workload (p_hidden = 0.739); session-cumulative coverage reaches 99.8 percent only of SELF-REPORTED references, and the same paper measures self-report over-claiming at 32-49 percent, deflating genuine causal coverage to at most about 70 percent. It further reports that on single-shard collaborative writing the mechanism is actively harmful (100 percent contradicted final states under ORI-ON in that regime, versus 85.6 percent under ORI-OFF), and that HTTP/2 multiplexing breaks its core ordering assumption. An RFA resident is a strictly worse observation target: its Read/Grep/Glob traffic on the pack directory is harness-internal and never reaches the hub (`src/resident.ts:609` documents that `canUseTool` is never consulted for harness-internal tools). Hub-side read-set reconstruction over pack workspaces is rejected. The PARKED half survives because the failure is observational, not conceptual: on surfaces where the hub IS the storage engine (task board, room state, a future hub-owned shared document), every read is a hub call, p_hidden is zero by construction, and per-key OCC with version-check-at-commit is cheap and SDK-free. That is also S-Bus's dedicated-shard topology restated: one writer per shard plus observable shared reads, which is the ownership-over-locking shape rung 6 already wants.

## 4. Transactions for agent actions: compensation and consumption, not isolation

`papers/sagallm-transaction-guarantees.pdf` (arXiv:2503.11951, VLDB 2025, fetched 2026-08-25) adapts the Saga pattern: explicitly relax atomicity and isolation, make every consequential step compensable, and validate with agents independent of the planner (state tracked along application, operation, and dependency dimensions). What it actually evaluates is narrow: four planning problems from the REALM benchmark under injected disruptions across four LLMs (March 2025), showing standalone LLM planners silently drop constraints during replanning while the saga-structured system preserves them. Read it as a reference shape with thin benchmarking, not as scale evidence. The shape fits RFA's gated acting tools exactly: an approval card that admits an action should carry the action's compensator, because rung 5's copy-on-write covers files and nothing covers a Linear project already created when the run's second action fails.

`papers/caplease-durable-authorization.pdf` (arXiv:2608.01710, fetched 2026-08-25) is the sharpest miss the ladder has. Its measured base rate: across 10,152 agent trajectories with uncertain execution outcomes, 39.8 percent produced semantically equivalent re-proposals of an already-executed action; lost acknowledgements induced re-proposals in 58.0 percent of cases, timeouts 46.0 percent, ambiguous results 31.0 percent, delegation or restart 24.0 percent. Single-use tokens do not help because the retry legitimately obtains a FRESH token for the same authorization. The fix is durable state over the authorization instance: a canonical action identity sigma binding tool, arguments, and resource; a budget b; atomic Issued-Prepared-Committed transitions over a ledger; and an idempotency key the external sink must honor (their transactional tests: 0 counterexamples across 282 actions including 28,200 two-thread races; the path costs 0.92 ms average). RFA today: every gated call passes `canUseTool` (`src/resident.ts:623`), so each materialization needs a fresh human approval, which already beats the paper's identifier-local baseline. But there is no canonical-action identity ACROSS cards and no idempotency contract for the tool behind the card (grep for dedup/idempotency in `src/bridge.ts` returns nothing, 2026-08-25), so the human is the ledger: after a crash mid-turn or a lost tool acknowledgement, the model re-proposes, a second identical card appears, and a human who approves both has authorized two executions of one intent. Bypass-mode packs (`src/resident.ts:632`) have no consumption control at all. Parallel runs sharing one identity multiply exactly the retry, resume, and delegation paths the paper measures. Recommendation 4 is the smallest CapLease borrowing that closes this: hash a canonical form of (tool, args, resource) into the card, refuse to auto-present a card whose sigma has an unconsumed or committed sibling within a window, and pass an idempotency key to the acting tool.

## 5. Scheduling: the account cap is the shape Autellix attacks

`papers/autellix-agent-serving.pdf` (arXiv:2502.13965, fetched 2026-08-25) measures that FCFS at the LLM-call level causes head-of-line blocking for agent programs, because a program is a chain (or DAG) of calls and any wait multiplies across the chain. Its schedulers (PLAS for chains, ATLAS for DAGs) are non-clairvoyant: they prioritize by attained service, the program's cumulative completed decode time, discretized to avoid thrashing, with an anti-starvation promotion when wait-to-service ratios exceed a bound; measured 4-15x throughput at equal latency over vLLM-class FCFS, with intra-program cache hit rates above 90 percent favoring keeping a program's calls on one engine. RFA's admission is one layer up (leases on whole turns, `src/account.ts:228-268`) but the finding transfers: the current design has NO queue by explicit comment ("There is no queue: a denied caller retries", `src/account.ts:37`), with static lane reserves (serve 0, schedule 1, background 2, `src/account.ts:40`). Under contention, grant order among retriers is a race, so a run three turns into a five-turn task has no preference over a run that has not started; that is priority inversion between a nearly-finished investment and a fresh arrival, and it is invisible today only because the cap is 3 and runs are serial per resident. The in-process turn lock is honest FIFO (`src/turnlock.ts:15-28`). Recommendation 8 parks the fix on the right trigger with the CLAUDE.md alerting rule attached: a starvation meter must state what it reads at zero traffic.

Within-turn concurrency: `papers/asyncfc-futures-function-calling.pdf` (arXiv:2605.15077, fetched 2026-08-25) achieves decode/execution overlap with futures and dependency-aware scheduling, no model changes and the standard function-calling protocol preserved, using declared per-tool read/write annotations over a path-labeled state tree and conservative serialization by default; measured 1.26x (BFCL v4 web search) to 1.44x (SWE-bench Lite with SWE-agent, scaled latencies) with accuracy statistically unchanged. Two lessons: the gain from within-turn async is real but bounded well under 2x, and it is an execution-layer property RFA does not own (the Claude SDK runs the tool loop). Rung 2 stands; RFA's unit of parallelism is the run, not the tool call.

## 6. The missing rung: candidate parallelism, and selection as the real cost

`papers/large-language-monkeys.pdf` (arXiv:2407.21787, fetched 2026-08-25) established that coverage (any-sample-correct) scales close to log-linearly with sample count across four orders of magnitude: SWE-bench Lite from 15.9 percent at one sample to 56 percent at 250 (DeepSeek-Coder-V2 + Moatless), beating the then-SOTA single attempt of 43 percent. The same paper shows the catch: on domains without automatic verifiers, common selection methods (majority vote, reward models) plateau around 100 samples and forfeit most of the coverage. `papers/codemonkeys-swe-test-time.pdf` (Stanford, 2025-01-24, fetched 2026-08-25) turns that into a system with a measured bill: 10 parallel (edit, test) state machines per issue, model-written tests for voting plus a dedicated selection state machine, 69.8 percent coverage, 57.4 percent after selection (11.6 points lost to selection alone), about 2,291 USD for all of SWE-bench Verified; context retrieval amortizes across candidates at under 15 percent of cost. `papers/dei-diversity-swe-agents.pdf` (arXiv:2408.07060, fetched 2026-08-25) measures the committee version over heterogeneous agents: a group averaging 26.6 percent individually has a 54.3 percent oracle union, and an LLM review committee recovers 34.3 percent, a 25 percent relative lift over the best member's 27.3 percent. DEI also documents INTRA-agent diversity: ten runs of the same agent (Agentless, Moatless, Aider) with identical parameters resolve materially different issue sets, which is the evidence that identical RFA packs, not just heterogeneous ones, produce useful candidate spread.

The ladder has no rung for this, and it is the cheapest concurrency RFA can ship: N runs of one task in N scratch dirs or worktrees that NEVER merge, one output selected, the rest deleted. Every conflict mechanism in this note becomes moot for that rung by construction. What it needs instead is a selector, and RFA is unusually well placed because the task board already separates completion from verification authority (wire 10.4): the selection step is a verification act by a member that did not generate the candidates. The costs to write into the spec honestly: expect to lose 10-15 points of coverage to selection without an executable check (CodeMonkeys), expect selection quality to depend on having something runnable (Monkeys), and expect the account cap to be the real limiter (N candidates compete for the same `max_inflight` 3, `src/account.ts:43`).

## 7. Pricing rung 5: merge-back conflicts are frequent and not small

`papers/agenticflict-merge-conflicts.pdf` (arXiv:2604.03551 v2, AIware 2026, fetched 2026-08-25) ran deterministic merge simulation on 107,026 real AI-coding-agent PRs from 59,412 repositories (from the AIDev dataset): 27.67 percent produce textual conflicts. A conflicting PR averages 4.36 files (median 2), 11.36 conflict regions, 540 conflict lines. Rates vary by agent (Copilot 15.24 percent, Cursor 19.75 percent, Devin 22.85 percent, Claude Code 25.93 percent on n=779, OpenAI Codex 31.85 percent) and rise steeply with churn: about 9.9 percent at 2-line median churn, near 30 percent at 25 lines, saturating at 32-33 percent. Caveats for the transfer: this measures agent-PR-versus-advanced-base-branch, not two RFA runs racing in one pack, and textual conflicts only, so logical conflicts (the incompatible-design-choice failure that state-management critiques of worktree isolation name) are on top of these numbers, not inside them. Directionally both caveats push the same way: rung 5's real conflict rate for concurrent runs editing one pack will not be "rare". Recommendation 3 writes the numbers into the rung; recommendation 1 changes what happens when they hit.

## 8. Rung 6 prior art, and what rung 1 actually needs

`papers/atm-prewrite-admission.pdf` (arXiv:2607.00041, fetched 2026-08-25) is direct prior art for rung 6's board-refuses-intersecting-claims: structured write intents with declared scopes, a broker that admits, composes, serializes, or fail-closes BEFORE any governed mutation lands, a neutral steward as the sole applier, and drift epochs invalidating stale task direction. Read with eyes open: single author, evaluation is a 12-scenario deterministic matrix plus a three-week single-adopter study, no comparative baselines, and the paper itself scopes its claim to feasibility. Take the shape (admission at claim time, ownership at assignment, fail-closed on undeclared overlap), not the evidence. It composes cleanly with RFA's existing claim fence: the claim token keyed by room, task and attempt (`src/store.ts:451-458`) and the claimable-state check (`src/store.ts:2477-2480`) are the enforcement point a resource key extension would ride on.

`papers/governed-shared-memory-multi-agent.pdf` (arXiv:2606.24535, fetched 2026-08-25) names four failure modes of shared agent memory measured against a live multi-tenant service: unauthorized leakage, stale propagation, contradiction persistence, provenance collapse. The relevant result for rung 1: contradiction handling required temporal supersession as a first-class write semantic, and their live evaluation found a synchronous near-duplicate gate rejecting contradictory writes BEFORE the asynchronous contradiction detector ever saw them, i.e. ordering inside the memory pipeline mattered, not just exclusion. An in-process write lock on `src/memoryfs.ts` operations serializes the file edits and prevents none of the four failure modes; two serialized runs still write contradictory facts that coexist. Rung 1 is necessary (torn writes are real) but must not be sold as memory safety (recommendation 6; the memory dimension of this wave owns the mechanism).

## 9. Coordination languages after Linda: one genuinely new item

Per the wave-01 boundary, tuple spaces are not re-covered here. The one genuinely new coordination-medium result is `papers/blackboard-multi-agent-data-science.pdf` (arXiv:2510.01285 v2, Google Cloud AI Research + UMass, fetched 2026-08-25): a main agent posts requests to a shared blackboard and subordinate agents SELF-SELECT whether to respond based on their own capability, with responses on a separate board to avoid cross-influence; no controller holds a capability model of the workers. Measured 13-57 percent relative end-to-end improvement over the best baseline (including master-slave multi-agent) on KramaBench and discovery-augmented DSBench/DA-Code, and up to 9 percent relative F1 gain on data discovery. This is architectural validation for RFA's existing posture (capability-based roster discovery plus a task board agents claim from, rather than an orchestrator routing by a stale capability model), not a reason to build anything new. Nothing found in 2024-2026 resurrects full tuple-space semantics for LLM agents.

## What this dimension could not verify

Five scouted papers were not downloaded and read, so their claims are unweighed here and nothing in this note rests on them: DPBench (arXiv:2602.13255, contention-outcome benchmarking claimed to favor explicit ordering rules), Resume Means Resume (arXiv:2608.03836, conformance checking of framework resume semantics, directly relevant to the crash-recovery-mid-merge gap), Atomix (arXiv:2602.14849, transactional tool use), Continuum (arXiv:2511.02230, KV-cache TTL scheduling), and M1-Parallel (arXiv:2507.08944, whole-team run-level parallelism). AsyncLM (arXiv:2412.07017) is characterized here only through AsyncFC's published comparison (interrupt tokens plus fine-tuning required), not from its own PDF. Within the read papers: the deer-flow lost-update connection in arXiv:2606.17182 rests on that paper's account of an open issue, not on an independent reproduction; SagaLLM's evaluation covers four REALM planning problems, so its architecture is adopted here as a shape, not as scale evidence; ATM's evaluation is self-hosted with no baselines; and AgenticFlict measures agent-versus-branch conflicts on GitHub, which approximates but does not equal two RFA runs in one pack directory, and it counts textual conflicts only. The position paper's Table 1 numbers (63.3 versus 55.5 percent worktree isolation, 67.1 percent concurrency-attributable failures) are that paper's citations of third-party benchmarks and were not re-verified at their original sources.

## Open questions this dimension leaves

1. Reentrancy: no read paper addresses an agent's turn transitively asking the same agent (A's turn enqueues a question to A, which waits on A's turn lock, `src/turnlock.ts:15-28`). The FIFO queue makes this a deadlock by construction once the ask path is synchronous. Needs a repo-level answer, not literature.
2. What is the selection mechanism for candidate parallelism on non-code tasks, where CodeMonkeys-style executable tests do not exist? Monkeys shows verifier-free selection plateaus; DEI's committee recovers only about half the union. This decides how far recommendation 2 reaches beyond SWE packs.
3. Does CoAgent's notify-and-repair hold up when the conflicting surface is a git worktree diff rather than its registered tool state? Nobody has published that combination; it is exactly RFA's rung 5 plus recommendation 1.
4. The memory-merge-on-replica-reconvergence question the brief raises stays open: governed shared memory gives supersession semantics for one store, but no read paper merges two DIVERGED agent memories.

### A. Resume Means Resume (arXiv 2608.03836 v3, 2026-08-08): resume is not exactly-once anywhere, and the fix binds at the read path

`papers/resume-means-resume-agent-interruption.pdf` (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03836) defines a six-property conformance contract over the checkpoint/interrupt/resume surface of agent workflow frameworks (prefix continuation, effect exactly-once, fork determinism, checkpoint validity, consume-once split into consumption-count and effect-inertness, recovery determinism, plus a fork-intent protocol obligation), machine-checks it (TLC exhaustive to 7.4x10^6 distinct states, 196 TLAPS obligations discharged unbounded), and then measures five frameworks at pinned releases with a deterministic, LLM-free harness. The measured record is damning and specific: LangGraph 1.2.9 durably records a second resume value and never consults it (issue #6663, stable across five releases, mechanism located in source), silently persists schema-invalid state, and is exactly-once across interrupts but at-least-once across real SIGKILL on the same API; CrewAI 1.15.2 re-executes completed effect-bearing methods against its own written claim, with the duplicate invisible in framework state and visible only in the external effect ledger (state rebuilt from initial values reads "11" where exactly-once predicts 11, yet the effect fired twice); LlamaIndex Workflows documents at-least-once prefix replay; pydantic-graph 1.107.1 cannot resume at all after a mid-node crash (safety by unrecoverability); no two probed frameworks share a conformance profile (Table 7, p. 15).

The result that transfers hardest to RFA is the cross-process consume-once failure. Two OS processes resuming one parked interrupt fire the gated effect twice in 10/10 repetitions on both durable backends including live PostgreSQL; sweeping racer count and jitter (probe 168, Table 6, p. 14) shows every racer arriving within the window consumes, saturation 1.0 in 36 of 40 cells, no ceiling observed below k=16 (sixteen fires from one human approval), and the window's width tracks the gated node's own execution duration, i.e. the length of a model turn. Row-level locking and MVCC do not close it because interrupt consumption is a read-modify-write nobody made atomic (p. 14). Two racers on separate hosts over a WAN reproduce it 10/10 (probe 174, p. 15). The repair geography is the design lesson: a write-path gate (claims table before accepting the pending write) was built and FALSIFIED, still duplicating 10/10, because the serving loop decides from what the durable-state read returns; the repair that works takes the consumption claim at the durable-state READ path with one INSERT under a uniqueness constraint in the shared store, refusing the loser with a typed conflict before any node executes ({1:10} on both backends, p. 17).

For RFA this lands in three places. First, recommendation 4 gains a placement constraint: CapLease says an approval needs a canonical action identity and a consumption ledger; this paper proves per-process sequencing of that ledger does not compose (the sequential stray-resume case is inert while the identical delivery from a second process fires, probe 159 vs 126), so the consumption record for gated actions must live in a SHARED durable store and be claimed by compare-and-swap at read time, before execution. RFA's shape is exactly the hazard's shape: an approval card is a parked interrupt, a resident is a process the supervisor restarts, and a parallel-run future multiplies deliveries into the window. Second, the paper is direct evidence for the crash-recovery-mid-merge gap the brief raises (open question left by the body): recovery determinism, the property that skip-versus-re-execute is a pure function of the durable log, is exactly what a merge-back interrupted by SIGKILL needs stated, and the paper shows deployed systems drift on it under release churn when it is unstated (regressions #7361/#6792 shipped inside 1.1.x). Third, a scoping caveat carried honestly: this is a single-author preprint, the artifact is private pending publication, and the probed planes are Python orchestration frameworks, not the Claude Agent SDK that RFA sits on; the transfer is by shape (RFA must treat SDK session resume semantics as unverified until probed, and own its consumption records rather than inheriting whatever the SDK does).

### B. Atomix (arXiv 2602.14849 v2, 2026-05-29): effect classes and gate-until-commit, and abort is cheap at action granularity

`papers/atomix-durable-agent-execution.pdf` (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.14849, MPI-SWS/Aarhus/EPFL, code released) adds transactional settlement at the tool boundary: adapters record read scopes and effects, the transaction seals its footprint, and commit fires only when per-resource frontiers certify no earlier conflicting work can still arrive. Effects are classified (reversible, reversible-with-cost, bufferable, irreversible-gated, optionally idempotent-known-outcome); irreversible effects are held at the adapter gate and fire only on commit; abort compensates in reverse dependency order. Measured, with a mechanism-matched 9-baseline harness: under fault injection on tau-bench retail (GPT-4.1, fp=0.30) Tx-Full keeps 57 percent clean success while every non-checkpoint baseline falls to 0-7 percent, and Checkpoint-Replay is statistically TIED on recovery alone (53 percent, Fisher p=1.0, an honest reported null); the differentiation is elsewhere. Under 4-agent forced overlap Tx-Full holds 0 invariant violations at 0 ms wait while Workflow-Lock pays 112 ms (scaling to ~2.4 s per call at 64 agents) and OCC stays clean only by ~3,000 rejected commits per 100 runs (Table 1, p. 7). On irreversible-effect leakage against a real append-only sink, gating wins outright: Tx-Full 0/500 leaked sends versus Saga-Compensation 400/500 (compensation cannot un-send), Checkpoint-Replay 200/500 (retries re-externalize), misclassification ablation 300/500 (Table 2, p. 7). Overhead is 7.7 microseconds per step and 17 LOC per adapter. Limits stated plainly: single-process prototype, crash-safe dedup unit-tested only, and adapter metadata is safety-critical with an asymmetric failure mode: only over-broad scopes fail closed; too-narrow scopes produce 66/200 invariant violations and a wrong effect class leaks 17/200 (Table 21, p. 23).

Three transfers. (1) The approval card should carry an EFFECT CLASS, and irreversible actions should gate until settlement, not compensate after: the 0/500-versus-400/500 spread is the measured argument that the SagaLLM compensator shape the body adopts in section 4 is the right fallback for reversible-with-cost effects and the wrong primary for irreversible ones. This extends recommendation 4's content; the verdict was already DECIDED. (2) Atomix qualifies the CoAgent framing behind recommendation 1 without flipping it: abort-and-retry is Pareto-best HERE because the transaction is an action phase (a read-plan-write span, seconds), so an abort discards little reasoning; CoAgent's anti-abort numbers were at turn granularity, and rung 5's merge-back is run-sized. Recommendation 1 stands for run-granularity conflicts; if RFA ever settles effects at sub-run granularity, Atomix's sealed-footprint-plus-frontier shape is the measured alternative. (3) The interposition point maps: Atomix's adapter shim is RFA's `canUseTool`, and its frontier-advancement contract (premature advancement produced 200/200 out-of-order commits, Table 18, p. 22) is a hub-side responsibility, consistent with rung 6's ownership-at-the-board stance. Scope declarations should err broad.

### C. DPBench (arXiv 2602.13255 v2, 2026-06-03): fast weigh

`papers/dpbench-data-parallel-agents.pdf` (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.13255, UNC Pembroke, code released). Measured: Dining Philosophers as a Dec-POMDP over five frontier LLMs plus a uniform-random baseline. At N=5, simultaneous action, default prompt, no communication: deadlock 25 percent (GPT-5.2, whose CI overlaps the random baseline's 13.3 percent) to 90 percent (Gemini 2.5 Flash). Three structural interventions each drive the worst model from 90 percent to about zero: three rounds of pre-commitment communication (a SINGLE round does not help: 86.7 vs 90 percent), a one-paragraph resource-ordering or symmetry-breaking prompt (0.0 percent), or growing the group to N=10. Weighed honestly: the substrate is one classical toy with no tools and no real effects, n=20-30 per cell leaves wide Wilson CIs, and the headline ("the protocol determines the outcome, not the model") is supported within that toy only. What transfers is an empirical floor under rung 6 and recommendation 7's PARKED half: leaving contention to zero-shot agent negotiation fails at near-random rates, one message exchange is measurably insufficient coordination, and encoding the ordering rule in the protocol is what works: a board that refuses intersecting claims IS the resource-ordering intervention. No verdict changes; rung 6's ownership-over-locking stance gains its first controlled measurement.

### D. Continuum (arXiv 2511.02230 v6): fast weigh

`papers/continuum-llm-serving.pdf` (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02230, UC Berkeley/Stanford/Tensormesh/Tsinghua, on vLLM). Measured: KV-cache time-to-live pinning across tool calls plus program-level FCFS yields 1.12x-3.66x average job-delay reduction and 1.10x-3.22x throughput over vLLM and an Autellix reimplementation (Llama-3.1 8B/70B, Gemma-3 12B, three hardware setups); the abstract's "over 8x" is the best case on Tensormesh's internal testbed, not the standard harness. The workload characterization is the useful part for RFA: SWE-agent traces average 10.9 turns with mean tool time 925 ms, tool durations are heavily long-tailed (the slowest 10 percent of one tool accounts for 94.1 percent of its total delay), and remaining work shrinks predictably with turns served, which is why program-level FCFS approximates shortest-remaining-time-first. Weighed honestly: this is inference-engine-layer scheduling; RFA does not operate an inference engine (the model side is the Anthropic API) and has no lever over KV cache. It refines the shape of recommendation 8's PARKED grant-order design (prefer attained service, keep a program's turns together, bound retention because tool durations are long-tailed) and changes no verdict; recommendation 10's boundary (within-turn and engine-level concurrency are not RFA's business) is reinforced, not challenged.

### E. M1-Parallel (arXiv 2507.08944, ICML 2025): fast weigh

`papers/m1-parallel-multi-agent.pdf` (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08944, Microsoft Research). Measured, on GAIA with GPT-4o and Magentic-One teams, 5 runs per task: running N whole teams on the SAME task in parallel with early termination (first finisher wins) gives 1.6-1.8x end-to-end speedup at 3 teams and 1.8-2.2x at 5 while maintaining completion rate, at 1.7-2.6x cost; aggregation mode instead improves completion (+3, +5, +1 tasks at levels 1/2/3 out of 53/86/26 at three teams) at higher latency and roughly 2.5-3.4x cost. LLM-based aggregation beats majority voting but a measured gap to best-of-k oracle selection remains, and explicitly prompting for plan DIVERSITY shows no gain over repeated high-temperature sampling (Figure 9: repeated planning completes more tasks at less cost). Weighed honestly: one benchmark family, one model, and level-3 counts are small (single-digit solves), so the aggregation deltas are thin; the latency results are the robust part. This is run-level candidate parallelism, exactly recommendation 2's rung, measured OFF the SWE domain: it partially answers open question 2 (the selection gap persists on non-code tasks; an LLM committee recovers some but not all of the union, consistent with DEI), and it adds the selector-free variant the body missed: when any verified completion is acceptable, early-stop needs no selector at all and buys latency rather than coverage. The diversity null supports the brief's replicas-as-distinct-members shape with IDENTICAL packs: no diversity engineering required. Recommendation 2 stays DECIDED, reinforced.
